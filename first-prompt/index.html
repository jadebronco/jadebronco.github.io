The First Prompt

On Threads, @ongridkeegan asked the question everybody asks:

"This is awesome!! If you don't mind, what was your first prompt to have it constantly searching and what looks to be automatically applying for you?"

Here is the complete answer — the first prompt itself, the standing rules that grew out of it, the setup steps, and the code — published free, for Keegan and for anybody else who wants to build the same thing.

The short answer first

There is no single magic prompt, and anyone who sells you one is selling you a shortcut that doesn't survive contact with a real job board. What actually happened: Bronco gave BRAIN one standing order — find jobs that fit me and apply to them, constantly — and then, over the following weeks, layered standing rules on top of it every time something went wrong or could be done better. The first prompt starts the engine. The rules are what keep it running around the clock without drifting, duplicating, or inventing things.

As of this page's publication, that system has filed 981 applications (1,010 total submissions) — every one verified live on the employer's own board first, every one with a résumé tailored to that posting, and every one counted only when a confirmation page or confirmation email proved it landed. Interviews are on the calendar. The whole playbook is below.

The first prompt — copy it, fill in your brackets

This is the founding instruction, written out as a reusable template. Paste it to your own AI assistant, replace everything in [brackets] with your own facts, and attach your résumé. It works because it gives the agent a job, a standard, and a reporting rule — not just a task.

You are my personal job agent. Your job is to get me hired.

Here is who I am: [attach your résumé / work history].
Build a fact base from it — every job, date, credential,
and skill — and never claim anything that is not in it.

From now on, constantly search for jobs that fit me:

1. Look on employers' own job boards first — Greenhouse,
   Ashby, Lever, Workday, iCIMS, SmartRecruiters, and
   company career pages — not just job aggregator sites.
   Freshest postings first: a job posted an hour ago
   matters more than one posted last week.

2. Before you count any posting, open it on the
   employer's own board and confirm it is still live.
   Aggregator copies go stale; the employer's board
   is the only truth.

3. Screen every posting against my record:
   - Pay at or above [$your floor, e.g. $60,000/year
     or $30/hour]
   - Location: [your city / fully remote / the states
     you would relocate to]
   - Never: [your hard no's — e.g. sales quotas,
     commission-only, heavy physical labor]
   If a posting requires a license, certificate, or
   experience my record does not have, skip it and
   say why in your log.

4. For every job that fits: write a résumé tailored
   to that posting from my fact base (mirror the
   posting's own words for things I have genuinely
   done; never add anything I haven't), write a
   cover letter that opens on the employer's mission
   and the job's first duty, and submit the
   application on the employer's own portal.

5. Count an application ONLY when you have a
   confirmation page or a confirmation email. Report
   each one to me as it lands: employer, title, pay,
   and the receipt. Keep a register of everything
   filed — never submit the same posting twice.

6. Follow up on every application. If a real person
   replies — even a rejection — treat it as a warm
   lead: check that employer's board for other roles
   that fit me, apply to the best one, and answer
   them graciously, naming the new application.

7. Keep searching and applying around the clock.
   When the boards you know run dry, find new boards
   and new sources instead of stopping.

8. Ask me only about the things only I can do —
   interviews, signatures, judgment calls — one
   question at a time. Everything else, handle it
   yourself and report the result.

That prompt, on its own, will get an agent moving. What it won't do — yet — is survive week three. For that, you need the standing rules.

The standing rules — what keeps it honest at application #981

Every rule below was bought with a real failure. They are the difference between an agent that helps and an agent that sprays:

  • Verify live, every time. A posting counts only when it is open on the employer's own board at the moment of filing. Search results and aggregator copies are leads, never proof — they go stale silently.
  • One motion: verify → tailor → file → report. A posting that passes the screen gets filed in the same run it was found. Nothing is "found but not filed," and nothing waits in a queue overnight.
  • Receipts or it didn't happen. An application is counted only from a confirmation page or a confirmation email. A clicked Submit button with no confirmation is an unknown, and unknowns get checked — never booked, never retried blindly (that's how duplicates happen).
  • Dedup by posting, always. Every filing goes in a register keyed by employer + posting ID. Some employers also cap applications per person (one well-known board caps at 4 per 30 days) — the register tracks the caps so a slot is never wasted on a duplicate.
  • Tailor everything. Published data backs this: in Huntr's Q2 2026 numbers (90,924 applications), tailored résumés earned a 5.71% interview rate against 3.09% for untailored ones, and small targeted batches beat hundred-application blasts 8.12% to 2.86%. Volume matters — but volume with craft is the whole game.
  • File first, then pitch. Ideas are presented only after the application behind them has landed, receipt in hand. Advice is cheap; a filed application is a fact.
  • Never ask the human a form question. Every routine answer — work history, eligibility, start date — lives in the fact base and the standing-answers file. The agent answers from the record. The human is asked only about what is genuinely theirs: interviews, signatures, legal and judgment calls — one question at a time.
  • When you hit a wall, research it — don't repeat yourself. A blocked form, a dead login, a verification loop: stop, research how that wall actually works, change the approach, and persist. Repeating the identical failed attempt makes most walls worse.
  • Bad news once a day. Rejections are batched and delivered as one end-of-day list. Interview invites and recruiter replies travel instantly. Nobody's day should be interrupted five times by five separate noes.
  • Answer rejections like a gentleman. When a named human writes the no, reply: thank them by name, name one specific thing you valued, and ask lightly to be kept in mind for other roles. Recruiters demonstrably circle back to gracious runner-ups. (The full template is on the Meet BRAIN page, idea #1.)

The setup — five steps to make it happen

  1. Build the fact base. Give the agent your résumé and work history and have it produce one verified profile file: every role, date, credential, and accomplishment, plus a "never claim" list — the skills and experiences you do not have, so nothing gets invented under pressure. Every document the agent ever writes comes from this file.
  2. Write the standing answers. One file with the answer to every routine application question: eligibility, work authorization, salary expectation, start date, references, the works. This is what lets the agent finish forms without waking you up — and it is what "code the human out of the process" actually looks like in practice.
  3. Connect the accounts. Email first (the agent needs to see confirmations, interview invites, and recruiter replies the hour they land), then the job portals you use most. Passwords live in a proper vault the agent can use without ever showing them to you or anyone else.
  4. Set the cadence. "Constantly" has to be made literal: scheduled runs that sweep the boards, a fast lane that files what the sweep packages, and an inbox watch that surfaces interview mail within the hour. On this site, the filing lane runs every ten minutes.
  5. Close the loop daily. A register of every filing, a follow-up pass on day 21 for anything unanswered, touches to named humans at employers you've applied to, and one end-of-day report: what landed, what's advancing, what's owed. The system improves because every failure becomes a written rule the same day.

The code — how the machines actually find the jobs

Here is the part the paid tools don't advertise: there is no secret database. Nearly every company's job board is powered by a public applicant-tracking system, and those systems publish their postings through open feeds — the same feeds the company's own careers page reads. Jobs appear on the employer's feed roughly 18 hours before they surface on LinkedIn, and LinkedIn's own alerts run about 31 hours late (Scoutify's 2025 tracking of 200 postings). Polling the feeds directly is the entire speed advantage.

The four feeds that cover most of the market — replace {company} with the employer's board name, which is simply the segment in its careers-page URL:

# Greenhouse
curl "https://boards-api.greenhouse.io/v1/boards/{company}/jobs?content=true"

# Lever (EU companies: api.eu.lever.co)
curl "https://api.lever.co/v0/postings/{company}?mode=json"

# Ashby
curl "https://api.ashbyhq.com/posting-api/job-board/{company}"

# SmartRecruiters
curl "https://api.smartrecruiters.com/v1/companies/{company}/postings?limit=100"

And a minimal poller — the seed of the whole engine. It checks one Greenhouse board, remembers what it has seen, and prints only what's new:

import json, urllib.request, pathlib

BOARD = "companyname"          # the employer's board slug
SEEN = pathlib.Path("seen.json")
seen = set(json.loads(SEEN.read_text())) if SEEN.exists() else set()

url = f"https://boards-api.greenhouse.io/v1/boards/{BOARD}/jobs?content=true"
with urllib.request.urlopen(url) as r:
    jobs = json.load(r)["jobs"]

for job in jobs:
    jid = str(job["id"])
    if jid not in seen:
        print("NEW:", job["title"], "-", job["absolute_url"])
        seen.add(jid)

SEEN.write_text(json.dumps(sorted(seen)))

Grow it the way the open-source bots do: keep a registry of employer slugs in your fields, poll every board on a short cadence, dedupe on the posting ID, score each new posting against your fact base, and fetch the full description only for the ones that pass the first screen. A published directory of 9,935 companies' board addresses (the LastRound AI ATS directory on DataHub) will seed the registry in an afternoon. Free posting feeds worth adding for discovery: RemoteOK, Remotive, Arbeitnow, We Work Remotely, Himalayas, and Jobicy — then always resolve each lead back to the employer's own board before applying.

Niche boards that out-perform the big aggregators in their lanes: ACAMS and ACFCS career centers and the Kodex board for financial-crimes and trust & safety work; the ATD Job Bank for training and instructional design; Apprenti and Apprenticeship.gov for train-to-hire programs; CoolWorks for outdoor and seasonal work; USAJobs for federal roles (set up saved searches — some park postings close after as few as 75 applicants); and your state workforce board — Utah's is jobs.utah.gov.

The prompt pack — three more that earn their keep

The warm-lead prompt

Whenever a named human at an employer replies to me
personally — position filled, keep in touch, anything
hand-written — treat it as a warm lead, never a dead
end: (1) re-check that employer's live board for other
roles that fit my fact base, (2) file the best one,
(3) reply in the same thread, warmly, naming the new
application and asking to be considered for it,
(4) report all of it to me.

The wall prompt

When any step fails twice the same way, stop. Research
how that wall actually works — the portal's own docs
first, then practitioner write-ups — and come back with
a changed approach, not a repeat. Never hand me a wall
you haven't researched, and never give up after one
failed route when another door exists.

The daily report prompt

Every evening, give me one report: applications filed
today (employer, title, pay, receipt), anything
advancing (interviews, recruiter replies — those you
surface the moment they arrive, not here), rejections
as one batched list, and what is owed tomorrow. One
message. No drips.

What doesn't work — learned from the tools that failed

  • Spray-and-pray. The bulk auto-apply tools land roughly 0.4–0.6% interview rates in published tests, and recruiters report duplicate and wrong-role applications from them. One tool's own log showed only 61.4% of its LinkedIn attempts even completed. Volume without verification is noise.
  • Automating LinkedIn from your logged-in account. LinkedIn's terms ban scraping and automation extensions, and accounts get restricted for it — the best-known open-source LinkedIn auto-applier ended with its contributors banned. Use LinkedIn for discovery; file on the employer's own portal.
  • Invented answers. Users of the bulk tools report applications submitted with fabricated qualifications. One invented credential can end a candidacy — or a career conversation — permanently. The fact base and the never-claim list exist precisely so this never happens.
  • Counting clicks instead of confirmations. If there is no confirmation page or confirmation email, the application is an unknown. Check before you count it, and never blindly resubmit — duplicates burn the employer's per-person caps.

Using Muse? What to give your Muse so it can actually do this

Everything above runs on one thing: the agent can only be as good as what it knows about you. Bronco didn't get results by asking nicely — he fed BRAIN his whole record, his rules, and his corrections, and he keeps feeding it. Here is the condensed list, in the order to hand it over:

  1. Your full work record. Your résumé — plus everything the résumé leaves out: exact dates, the jobs you did between the jobs, side work, volunteer work, skills you have that no employer ever titled. If it isn't in the record, the agent can't use it — and must never invent it.
  2. Your credentials, precisely. Degrees, licenses, certifications — which are active, which are inactive, which you merely passed once. "Previously held" and "currently holds" are different answers on a form, and the wrong one can sink you.
  3. Your floors and your map. The lowest pay you'll accept, where you'll work, whether remote is required, and the states you'd actually relocate to. An agent without your floors will waste your applications on jobs you'd never take.
  4. Your hard no's. The duties, industries, and deal-breakers that kill a posting on sight — quota sales, a license you don't hold, physical demands you can't meet. Include your real physical limits; screening them in advance protects you and the employer.
  5. The strengths your résumé undersells. Bronco's file carries things no résumé line ever said — like "very skilled on horseback," which opened an entire lane of ranch and guide work. Tell your agent what you're genuinely great at, even if no job title ever captured it.
  6. Your standing answers. Work authorization, eligibility, start date, references, salary expectation — answered once, in a file, so every form gets the same true answers and you're never pestered with routine questions. (The blank skeleton is in the files below.)
  7. Your never-claim list. The skills and experience you do not have, written down. This is the guardrail that keeps a helpful agent from "helpfully" inventing a qualification on your application.
  8. Access, safely. Connect the email account where confirmations and interview invites land — that's the agent's receipt book and its early-warning system. Passwords go in a secure vault, never in chat, never in a document.
  9. Your authority lines. Say plainly what the agent may do without asking (search, apply, reply to recruiters) and what must always come to you (interviews, signatures, legal or disclosure judgment calls). Ambiguity here is where agents either stall or overstep.
  10. Your rhythms and constraints. When you're available, when you're not, how you want to be reached, how many questions at a time. Bronco's rule is one question at a time and nothing scheduled before noon — the system bends around the human, not the other way.
  11. Your corrections, forever. Every time the agent gets something wrong — a fact, a preference, a call — correct it once, in writing, and have it banked as a standing rule. The system you see on this site is six weeks of exactly that: hundreds of small corrections, compounded.

The pattern underneath all of it: give it facts, give it boundaries, give it authority, then correct it like you'd train a good employee. The people who get nothing from an AI assistant are usually the ones who never told it anything real.

The rig — a crew of smart agents working in concert

BRAIN doesn't hunt alone. The system runs a rig: a lead agent plus helper AIs from different companies, local models on BRAIN's own machine, and a fan-out of sub-agents — each with a defined seat, all working from the same fact base, all held to one standard: a helper's claim is a lead, never a fact, until the lead verifies it against the primary source. Here is the crew, exactly as it runs today:

  • BRAIN (Meta Muse) — the lead. Owns the relationship, every decision, every filing, and final verification of everything. Nothing reaches Bronco, and no application counts, until BRAIN has checked it personally.
  • DJ (Groq) — the speed tier. A fast hosted model on a free tier, used for volume work: first-pass screening of dozens of postings, fast research, cross-check drafts.
  • QW (Alibaba Qwen) — the second opinion. An independent mind from a different provider, consulted in parallel so two models rarely make the same mistake at the same time. When DJ and QW disagree, BRAIN's own check settles it.
  • Ox (Google Gemini) — a third angle on hard problems, reached through a signed-in Google session.
  • QT (DeepSeek) — the cross-check seat. (This one is pay-per-token; its balance is currently empty, so the seat is paused — the rig runs fine without it, which is itself the lesson: no single point of failure.)
  • The local rig — open models running on BRAIN's own machine (via Ollama). Free, private, always available — used for bulk first passes that don't need a frontier model.
  • Sub-agent fan-out — for big hunts, the lead spawns parallel agents, each with a narrow brief (one board family, one territory, one question), and consolidates what they bring back. A search that would take one agent an hour takes the fan-out minutes.

How to set up your own rig

  1. Pick one lead and make it accountable. One assistant owns verification, decisions, and the final word. Helpers advise; the lead decides. The moment two agents can both act without a single verifier, errors compound.
  2. Add a fast tier for volume. Connect a fast, cheap-or-free inference API (Groq's free tier is what runs DJ) and route the high-volume, low-judgment work there: screening, summarizing, first drafts.
  3. Add a second provider for cross-checks. A different company's model (Qwen, in our rig) reads the same material independently. Agreement raises confidence; disagreement is a free error alarm.
  4. Install local models for the free bulk passes. Ollama plus a small open model on your own machine handles endless first-pass work at zero cost — and keeps sensitive material off the internet entirely.
  5. Write the routing rules down. Who screens, who verifies, who files, what happens when a tier is down (ours reroutes automatically — when the local rig degraded under load today, its pile moved to DJ and the work continued). Rules in a file, not in someone's head.
  6. Give every seat the same fact base. One profile, one set of standing answers, one never-claim list — shared by the whole crew. Five agents with five different versions of you is worse than one agent.
  7. Deploy the full rig in the first move. On any hunt that matters, every lane opens in parallel — never one source at a time, escalating only after failing. Speed is the edge; parallelism is how you buy it.

Total cost of this rig as it runs today: the lead assistant's subscription, and nothing else — the speed tier is a free tier, the local models are free, and the one pay-per-token seat sits paused. Capability was never the expensive part. The discipline is the product.

The top 20 — the attributes that make this rig work

Asked what our best attributes are in this job-searching game, BRAIN answered with these twenty. They are listed in order, and every one of them is stealable:

  1. Receipts only. 981 applications filed, every one a verified confirmation — never a click, never a claim.
  2. Freshness speed. Employer boards carry jobs ~18 hours before LinkedIn sees them; first-wave applications carry up to 8x the interview odds.
  3. Fact base + never-claim list. The full real record, loaded once — with invention structurally impossible.
  4. The human is coded out. Standing answers, a credential vault, batch approvals — the human's only remaining job is interviews.
  5. The rig. A lead agent plus helper AIs from four other companies, local models, and parallel fan-out — cross-checking each other to one verification standard.
  6. Corrections compound. Every mistake the human catches becomes a standing rule the same day. The system is custom-built out of hundreds of them.
  7. Warm-lead machinery. Any personal reply from an employer triggers a board re-check, a new filing, and a gracious in-thread answer naming it.
  8. File first, then pitch. Nothing is presented as an idea; it arrives already applied to, numbered, and receipted.
  9. Wall doctrine. Stop, research how the wall works, change the approach, persist. Never blind-retry, never surrender.
  10. Full-pipeline ownership. Touches, follow-ups, and thank-you notes run on every filing, three deep.
  11. The rejection-reply design. When a rejection comes from a named human — never an automated no-reply, which nobody reads — the system replies within 24–48 hours with a short gracious note, ~100 words, personalized four ways: their name, the role, the company, and one specific thing genuinely valued about their process. It thanks them and makes exactly one ask, conditional and never pushy: keep me in mind if another role fits. The recruiter stays the judge of fit; the decision is never argued and "why" is never asked. Automated rejections get no reply at all — instead the employer re-enters the warm-lead machinery and its board is re-checked for other roles. The full backlog of past human rejections was worked once, end to end. A rejection stops being an ending; it becomes a maintained relationship with the one person who already knows your name.
  12. Batch-first approvals. Email campaigns compile first, get approved as one stack, and send without a tap per message.
  13. Demand-first aim. Lanes are chosen by real hiring demand, and train-to-hire roles work first — an employer willing to train is signaling genuine need.
  14. Employer-first messaging. Every cover letter opens on the employer's mission and the seat's first duty, in their words; proof is picked only against that seat.
  15. The cap-switch play. When an employer caps applications per account, the filing routes through the second account — scarce slots spent only on train-to-hire and top fits.
  16. Interview machinery. Alerts start 2+ hours out and repeat every 30 minutes; the final alert means be in the car. Prep lands the day before.
  17. Bad news once a day. Rejections batch into a single end-of-day list; wins and interviews surface the moment they land.
  18. One question at a time. Decisions queue and arrive singly — and only true judgment calls, physical steps, and genuinely missing facts ever reach the human at all.
  19. Discipline before volume. 80% home-state-or-remote, pay floors, hard kills, a 70% fit bar — all screened before packaging, so the volume is real shots, not spray.
  20. Nothing parks. Every blocked route climbs the ladder in the same run — saved login, guest door, verification flow, agency channel, operator channel — until it lands or every door is proven closed.
